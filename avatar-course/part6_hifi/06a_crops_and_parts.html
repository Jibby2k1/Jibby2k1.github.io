Small parts need their own pixels and their own joints#

What you will learn

  1. Why a head or a hand comes out soft when the avatar is fitted to full-body frames, and how to count the pixels that explain it.

  2. How a crop camera renders one window of a frame at a higher resolution, and how a crop loss uses it.

  3. How a close-up take becomes a training row, although it shows only part of the body.

  4. Why a part with no joints of its own turns into a blob, and how extra joints with their own skinning weights fix it.

Prerequisites: Lesson 2b (the pinhole camera), Lesson 3b (linear blend skinning), Lesson 4b (sampling density and boost) and Lesson 5a (cu.fit).

1. Count the pixels#

A pinhole camera with focal length \(f\) (in pixels) draws an object of size \(s\) metres at depth \(z\) metres over

\[ n = \frac{f\,s}{z} \quad\text{pixels.} \]

A full-body frame has to fit about two metres of person into the image. The head is an eighth of that and a hand a tenth. So whatever the resolution of the frame, the head gets roughly an eighth of its height.

The cell below builds the capture for this lesson, a person turning on the spot, and measures \(n\) for the head and the hands. hifi.part_window returns a square window around a part: its centre is the projection of the part’s joint, and its side is the formula above with a margin.

actor = cu.ToyActor()
poses, trans = cu.motion("turn", 32)
cam = cu.orbit_camera(0, size=64)
capture = cu.make_video(actor, poses, trans, cam=cam)
train, test = capture.subset(range(0, 32, 2)), capture.subset(range(1, 32, 2))   # even frames train, odd frames test

z = float(cam.to_camera(torch.tensor([0.0, 1.6, 0.0]))[2])
for name, size_m in (("body", 1.75), ("head", 0.25), ("hand", 0.15)):
    print(f"{name:5s} {size_m:4.2f} m  ->  {cam.fx * size_m / z:5.1f} px of a {cam.width} px frame")

windows = {p: hifi.part_window(cam, poses[0], trans[0], p) for p in ("head", "l_hand", "r_hand")}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(3.6, 3.6))
ax.imshow(cu.to_np(capture.frame(0)), extent=(0, 64, 64, 0))
for p, (x0, y0, side) in windows.items():
    ax.add_patch(plt.Rectangle((x0, y0), side, side, fill=False, color=cu.COLORS["camera"], lw=1.8))
    ax.text(x0 + side / 2, y0 - 1 if p == "head" else y0 + side + 3.5, p, color=cu.COLORS["camera"], fontsize=8,
            ha="center")
ax.set_xlabel("u (px)"); ax.set_ylabel("v (px)"); ax.grid(False)
ax.set_title("one training frame and three part windows");

body  1.75 m  ->   54.2 px of a 64 px frame
head  0.25 m  ->    7.7 px of a 64 px frame
hand  0.15 m  ->    4.6 px of a 64 px frame

The head is a handful of pixels wide. The eyes and the mouth are smaller than one pixel each. The training loss compares pixels, so it cannot tell a sharp eye from a grey smudge of the same average colour.

The toy differs from a real capture in scale only. A real frame might be 4K and be shrunk to 512 px for training. Here the “full-resolution capture” is the actor, which we can render at any size, and the 64 px video is the shrunken training frame.

2. A crop camera, and the failure it reveals#

To look at the head closely we do not need a new camera position. We need the same camera, restricted to a window and sampled more finely. A window with corner \((x_0, y_0)\) viewed at zoom times the resolution maps pixel \((u, v)\) to \((u', v') = \text{zoom}\cdot(u - x_0,\; v - y_0)\). Insert the projection \(u = f_x\,x/z + c_x\):

\[ u' = \underbrace{\text{zoom}\,f_x}_{f_x'}\,\frac{x}{z} + \underbrace{\text{zoom}\,(c_x - x_0)}_{c_x'}, \qquad v' = \text{zoom}\,f_y\,\frac{y}{z} + \text{zoom}\,(c_y - y_0). \]

So cropping moves the principal point, zooming scales the focal length, and the rotation \(R\) and translation \(T\) do not change. That is all Camera.crop does. The next cell writes it out and checks two things: that it equals the library version, and that a crop render equals the same window cut out of a large render of the whole frame.

def crop_camera(cam, x0, y0, w, h, zoom):
    return cu.Camera(cam.R, cam.T, cam.fx * zoom, cam.fy * zoom, (cam.cx - x0) * zoom, (cam.cy - y0) * zoom,
                     int(round(w * zoom)), int(round(h * zoom)))

x0, y0, w, zoom = 24, 2, 16, 4                    # a head window on whole pixels, 4x zoom
mine, lib = crop_camera(cam, x0, y0, w, w, zoom), cam.crop(x0, y0, w, w, zoom)
assert (mine.fx, mine.cx, mine.cy, mine.width) == (lib.fx, lib.cx, lib.cy, lib.width)

big, _ = actor.render(cam.scaled(zoom), poses[0], trans[0])                 # the whole frame at 4x
cut = big[y0 * zoom:(y0 + w) * zoom, x0 * zoom:(x0 + w) * zoom]
crop, _ = actor.render(mine, poses[0], trans[0])                            # only the window, at 4x
diff = float((cut - crop).abs().max())
print(f"crop render vs. the same window of a {big.shape[0]} px render: max difference {diff:.1e}")
assert diff < 1e-3

crop render vs. the same window of a 256 px render: max difference 3.5e-06

The crop costs a sixteenth of the pixels of the big frame and shows the same picture. Now the failure. We fit an avatar to the 64 px frames, exactly as in Part 5, and look at it through the crop camera. To make sure the model could hold a face, it gets a finer texture and four times the sampling density on the head (Lesson 4b).

ITERS = 120
make_avatar = lambda seed=0: cu.ShellAvatar(tex_res=(24, 32), boost={"head": 4}, seed=seed)

# crop cameras and full-resolution head crops for the training and the held-out frames
crops_train = hifi.crop_targets(actor, train, "head", out_px=64)     # (cameras, rgb, mask)
crops_test = hifi.crop_targets(actor, test, "head", out_px=64)

def score(avatar):
    """Held-out scores: whole body frames, and the head seen through the crop camera."""
    return {"body": cu.evaluate(avatar, test)["mean_psnr"],
            "head crop": hifi.crop_psnr(avatar, *crops_test, test.poses, test.trans)}

body_only = make_avatar()
t0 = time.perf_counter()
cu.fit(body_only, train, iters=ITERS)
scores = {"body frames only": score(body_only)}
print(f"fit: {time.perf_counter() - t0:.0f} s   held-out PSNR: " +
      ", ".join(f"{k} {v:.1f} dB" for k, v in scores["body frames only"].items()))

fit: 3 s   held-out PSNR: body 37.6 dB, head crop 22.0 dB

def head_view(avatar, t=0):
    return avatar.render(crops_test[0][t], test.poses[t], test.trans[t])[0].detach()

truth_crop = lambda t=0: cu.composite_on(crops_test[1][t], crops_test[2][t])
cu.show_images([test.frame(0), truth_crop(), head_view(body_only), (head_view(body_only) - truth_crop()).abs().mean(-1)],
               ["held-out frame (64 px)", "capture, head crop", "body-only fit, head crop", "error"],
               size=2.3, vmax=0.4);

At body framing the fit scores well, and the frame looks right. Through the crop camera the face is a smear: the eyes and the mouth are missing or misplaced, although the texture has room for them. The data never asked for them.

3. The crop loss#

The fix is to put the missing pixels into the loss. Every step keeps the whole-frame loss of Part 5 and adds one crop:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_\text{frame}(\text{render}(K, \theta_t),\, I_t)}_{\text{64 px body frame}} \;+\; w_\text{crop}\,\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_\text{frame}(\text{render}(K^\text{crop}_s, \theta_s),\, I^\text{crop}_s)}_{\text{head window at full resolution}} . \]

\(K^\text{crop}_s\) is the crop camera of frame \(s\), and \(I^\text{crop}_s\) is the window cut from the full-resolution capture. The avatar, the pose \(\theta_s\) and the renderer are the same. Only the camera intrinsics and the target change, so there is nothing new to learn about geometry.

hifi.CropLoss is that second term, in the form cu.fit(extra_loss=...) expects. It draws its frame \(s\) from its own random stream, so the body frames that cu.fit draws are the same with and without the crop loss, and the two runs differ in one thing only.

W_CROP = 0.3
with_crop = make_avatar()
crop_loss = hifi.CropLoss(with_crop, *crops_train, train.poses, train.trans, weight=W_CROP)
t0 = time.perf_counter()
cu.fit(with_crop, train, iters=ITERS, extra_loss=crop_loss)
scores["+ crop loss"] = score(with_crop)
print(f"fit: {time.perf_counter() - t0:.0f} s, {crop_loss.calls} crop renders")
for name, s in scores.items():
    print(f"{name:18s} body {s['body']:.2f} dB   head crop {s['head crop']:.2f} dB")

gain = scores["+ crop loss"]["head crop"] - scores["body frames only"]["head crop"]
body_change = scores["+ crop loss"]["body"] - scores["body frames only"]["body"]
print(f"head crop {gain:+.1f} dB, body {body_change:+.2f} dB")
assert gain > 3.0, "the crop loss should clearly improve the head crop"
assert body_change > -1.5, "the body score should not collapse"

fit: 5 s, 120 crop renders
body frames only   body 37.63 dB   head crop 21.95 dB
+ crop loss        body 37.09 dB   head crop 31.92 dB
head crop +10.0 dB, body -0.54 dB

cu.show_images([truth_crop(0), head_view(body_only, 0), head_view(with_crop, 0),
                truth_crop(4), head_view(body_only, 4), head_view(with_crop, 4)],
               ["capture", "body frames only", "+ crop loss"] * 2, ncols=6, size=1.9);

The head crop improves by many decibels, on frames the fit never saw. The eyes and the mouth are in place and the hairline is clean. It is not as sharp as the capture: the texture and the Gaussian count still set a limit, but the limit is no longer the training pixels.

Now read the other column. The body score did not stay equal: it went down a little. The crop loss is not free. Each step now also pulls the head texels towards a 64 px target, and the same texels, seen through the body camera, are averaged over a pixel in a slightly different way. The step also costs more time, because it renders a second image. Both effects appear in the real project too (see below). The weight \(w_\text{crop}\) sets the trade: Exercise 1 asks you to move it.

4. Close-ups as training rows#

A crop only helps if the capture has the pixels. If the camera was four metres away, the full-resolution head is still small. The other source of pixels is a close-up take: the camera moves in and films the head alone.

A close-up frame shows a head and no body. It can still be a training row, because a training row needs only three things: an image, a camera and a body pose. The whole body model is placed in front of the close-up camera as usual. Most of it falls outside the image, and the renderer simply does not draw what is outside.

rows = list(range(0, 32, 4))                                         # eight close-up frames
close_cams = [hifi.closeup_camera(poses[t], trans[t], "head", size=64) for t in rows]
closeup = cu.make_video(actor, poses[rows], trans[rows], cam=close_cams)

def top_view(fig, theme):
    ax = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 1)
    _, joints = cu.forward_kinematics(poses[0], trans[0])
    cu.draw_skeleton(ax, joints, dims=(2, 1))
    for c, label in ((cam, "body camera"), (close_cams[0], "close-up camera")):
        eye = cu.to_np(c.center)
        half = 0.5 * c.height / c.fy                                 # tan of half the field of view
        fwd = cu.to_np(c.R[2]); up = -cu.to_np(c.R[1])
        for sgn in (-1, 1):
            far = eye + 5.0 * (fwd + sgn * half * up)
            ax.plot([eye[2], far[2]], [eye[1], far[1]], color=cu.COLORS["camera"], lw=1.2,
                    ls="-" if label.startswith("body") else "--")
        ax.scatter([eye[2]], [eye[1]], color=cu.COLORS["camera"], s=30, zorder=4)
        ax.annotate(label, (eye[2], eye[1]), xytext=(0, 8), textcoords="offset points", ha="center",
                    fontsize=8, color=theme["ink"])
    ax.set_xlim(-0.6, 4.6); ax.set_ylim(0, 2.2); ax.set_aspect("equal")
    ax.set_xlabel("z (m)"); ax.set_ylabel("y (m)"); ax.set_title("side view: two cameras, one body")
    ax2 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 2)
    ax2.imshow(cu.to_np(closeup.frame(0))); ax2.set_axis_off(); ax2.set_title("the close-up row (64 px)")

cu.themed(top_view, figsize=(8.2, 3.3), alt="Side view of the body with the body camera and the close-up camera, and a close-up frame")

The close-up camera’s view cone (dashed) covers the head and the shoulders. The legs are still there, in the model, below the frame. In the toy we know the body pose of the close-up frame. In a real capture nobody does: a body tracker cannot fit a body it cannot see, so a separate step has to place a body and a camera behind every close-up. That step is the hard part in practice, and its errors blur the head in the same way as the pose errors of Lesson 5b.

Training on both kinds of rows is one line: stack the two videos.

with_closeups = make_avatar()
both = hifi.concat_videos(train, closeup)
t0 = time.perf_counter()
cu.fit(with_closeups, both, iters=ITERS)
scores["+ close-up rows"] = score(with_closeups)
print(f"fit: {time.perf_counter() - t0:.0f} s on {len(train)} body rows + {len(closeup)} close-up rows")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.0))
names = list(scores)
x = np.arange(len(names))
ax.bar(x - 0.2, [scores[n]["body"] for n in names], 0.4, color=cu.COLORS["gaussian"], label="body frames (64 px)")
ax.bar(x + 0.2, [scores[n]["head crop"] for n in names], 0.4, color=cu.COLORS["camera"], label="head crop")
ax.set_xticks(x); ax.set_xticklabels(names); ax.set_ylabel("held-out PSNR (dB)"); ax.set_ylim(15, 45)
ax.legend(loc="upper right", ncols=2);
for n in names:
    print(f"{n:18s} body {scores[n]['body']:.2f} dB   head crop {scores[n]['head crop']:.2f} dB")
assert scores["+ close-up rows"]["head crop"] > scores["body frames only"]["head crop"] + 2.0

fit: 3 s on 16 body rows + 8 close-up rows
body frames only   body 37.63 dB   head crop 21.95 dB
+ crop loss        body 37.09 dB   head crop 31.92 dB
+ close-up rows    body 35.43 dB   head crop 29.12 dB

The close-up rows also sharpen the head, without any crop of the body frames. They cost body score for a plain reason: with the same number of steps, a third of the draws now go to rows that show no body. A real recipe gives the two kinds of rows separate draws, as the crop loss above does.

5. Parts without joints: the mitten hand#

Pixels are one half of the problem. The other half is motion. The toy skeleton ends at the wrist, like the SMPL body model [Loper et al., 2015] used by the standard pipeline, which has no finger joints and no jaw. Every Gaussian of the hand follows the wrist. When the real fingers curl, the model has no way to follow.

Recall linear blend skinning from Lesson 3b: a rest-pose point \(x_i\) moves to

\[\begin{split} x_i' = \sum_{j} w_{ij}\, A_j\, x_i, \qquad A_j = G_j\,G_j^{\text{rest}\,-1}, \qquad G_j = G_{\text{parent}(j)} \begin{bmatrix} R_j & t_j \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}. \end{split}\]

Nothing in these equations is specific to 17 joints. To graft a part rig we

  1. append new joints to the tree, as children of an existing joint (here the left wrist),

  2. give the points of the part weights \(w_{ij}\) on the new joints,

  3. feed the new joints’ rotations with the pose.

The old joints keep their indices and their transforms, so the rest of the body is untouched. hifi.ToyHand does this for a toy hand with three two-segment fingers: six new joints. It is a toy and nothing more: no learned shape, no thumb, capsules for fingers.

hand = hifi.ToyHand()
print(f"{len(cu.JOINTS)} body joints + {len(hand.parents) - len(cu.JOINTS)} finger joints:",
      ", ".join(hand.joint_names[len(cu.JOINTS):]))

# 1. the graft leaves the body alone: the first 17 skinning transforms are the body's own
body_pose, body_trans = cu.motion("dance", 8)
curls = torch.tensor([[0.9, 0.7], [0.2, 0.1], [1.2, 1.0]])            # (finger, [knuckle, middle joint]) in radians
A_grafted = hifi.skinning(hand.rest, hand.parents, hand.full_pose(curls, body_pose[3]), body_trans[3])
A_body, _ = cu.skinning_transforms(body_pose[3], body_trans[3])
print("largest change of a body transform:", float((A_grafted[:len(cu.JOINTS)] - A_body).abs().max()))
assert torch.allclose(A_grafted[:len(cu.JOINTS)], A_body, atol=1e-6)

# 2. the weights: palm on the wrist, each finger on its own two joints, blended at the middle joint
finger0 = hand.finger == 0
s = cu.to_np(hand.rest_points[finger0, 0] - hand.rest[len(cu.JOINTS), 0])
order = np.argsort(s)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 2.6))
ax.plot(100 * s[order], cu.to_np(hand.weights[finger0, len(cu.JOINTS)])[order], color=cu.COLORS["skeleton"],
        label="weight on the knuckle joint")
ax.plot(100 * s[order], cu.to_np(hand.weights[finger0, len(cu.JOINTS) + 1])[order], color=cu.COLORS["contact"],
        label="weight on the middle joint")
ax.set_xlabel("distance along the finger (cm)"); ax.set_ylabel("skinning weight"); ax.legend();

17 body joints + 6 finger joints: finger0_knuckle, finger0_mid, finger1_knuckle, finger1_mid, finger2_knuckle, finger2_mid
largest change of a body transform: 0.0

Now the experiment. The capture is the true hand opening and closing its fingers, each with its own timing, seen by two close-up cameras. Two models are fitted to it. Both have the same Gaussians in the same rest positions and learn a colour, an opacity and a size for each. They differ in one switch:

  • mitten: every Gaussian follows the wrist (the body skeleton as it is);

  • rig: the Gaussians follow the grafted finger joints, driven by the known finger pose.

hand_cams = hifi.hand_cameras(hand, size=48)
curls_train = hifi.finger_motion(12)
curls_test = hifi.finger_motion(6, phase=0.04)                       # poses between the training poses

models, hand_scores = {}, {}
for name, rig in (("mitten", False), ("rig", True)):
    models[name] = hifi.HandModel(hand, rig=rig)
    hifi.fit_hand(models[name], hand, curls_train, hand_cams, iters=200)
    hand_scores[name] = hifi.hand_psnr(models[name], hand, curls_test, hand_cams)
    print(f"{name:7s} held-out PSNR {hand_scores[name]:.1f} dB")
assert hand_scores["rig"] > hand_scores["mitten"] + 8.0

show = [0, 2, 4]
images = [hand.render(hand_cams[0], curls_test[t])[0] for t in show] \
       + [models["mitten"].render(hand_cams[0], curls_test[t])[0].detach() for t in show] \
       + [models["rig"].render(hand_cams[0], curls_test[t])[0].detach() for t in show]
titles = [f"capture, pose {t}" for t in show] + ["mitten"] * 3 + ["rig"] * 3
cu.show_images(images, titles, ncols=3, size=2.0);

mitten  held-out PSNR 20.0 dB

rig     held-out PSNR 46.3 dB

The mitten model gives the same picture for every pose, because nothing in it depends on the fingers. Its best answer under the loss is an average over the motion: solid where the hand always is (the palm), and a faint, muddy smear where a finger sometimes is. The three fingertip colours mix. No amount of resolution or crop loss repairs this, since the error is in the motion model and not in the texture.

The rigged model has the same number of Gaussians and the same learned quantities. With six more joints its fingers land where the captured fingers are, in poses it never saw.

One caveat belongs here. The rig moves the Gaussians where the finger pose says. In the toy that pose is exact. In a real capture it comes from a hand tracker, and a wrong finger pose puts a sharp finger in the wrong place. A rig turns “always blurred” into “as good as the tracking”.

From the real system#

All frames below are held-out close-ups of the author’s capture: the models never trained on them. “Standard track” is the body-only control, which never trained on close-up rows at all, so its close-ups show the effect of that missing data, not a capacity limit.

Face, standard track vs hi-fi (held-out talking take; drag the divider). Notice the two changes the hi-fi parts make: the crop loss lets the model resolve a face at all, and the face rig opens the mouth. Notice also what is still missing: the mouth opens less than in the capture, and eyes and skin are soft.

Hand, standard track vs hi-fi (held-out hands take). The finger rig and crop loss give the hand a shape and some finger structure, but the fingers are soft blobs in a different configuration from the capture. This is the project’s main open limitation: tracking and articulation errors dominate.

With and without the mouth proxy (held-out talking take, the frame with the largest jaw opening). Both models have the face rig, so both open the mouth; the proxy only adds a dark interior behind the lips. Notice how small the difference is. The held-out face score agrees: 24.67 dB without the proxy against 24.70 dB with it (results tour).

With and without face density (held-out expression take). Extra Gaussians on the face change little that the eye can see. The measured gain, +0.33 dB on the held-out face from one seed, is under twice the seed spread, so the repository reports it as not established.

In the real pipeline#

The real project calls this group of additions the hi-fi track. Each one is an opt-in configuration key on top of the body-only standard track.

Idea in this lesson

Real module

What is different there

crop camera and crop loss

gsmfit/hifi_crop.py, prep/hifi/prepare_crops.py

Crops of the head and both hands are cut from the native frames and stored with their windows. The loss is L1 + SSIM with a ramp, and a gate drops crops whose silhouette does not match.

close-up rows

gsmfit/closeup_place.py

A full SMPL body and a camera are fitted behind every close-up frame; they are not known.

finger joints

gsmfit/hand_rig.py, gsmfit/hand_hifi.py

The finger joints of MANO [Romero et al., 2017] (the SMPL+H hand) are grafted onto the SMPL deformer, with thinner shells and a higher density on the hands. Finger poses come from the HaMeR tracker [Pavlakos et al., 2024].

jaw and expressions

gsmfit/flame_rig.py, gsmfit/mouth_proxy.py

Every Gaussian of the head is attached to a FLAME head surface [Li et al., 2017] and follows its jaw and expressions. A learned mouth interior is shown when the jaw opens.

scores inside a window

eval/hifi/region_metrics.py

Face, hands and hair are scored at native crop resolution, in regions fixed by one reference model.

What the published records say, and where:

  • Why crops. Training shrinks the 1920 px canvas to 512 px, so a face is about 60 px and a hand about 40 px (docs/hifi_crop.md). The same record reports the trade you measured in Section 3: the crop loss raised the held-out face at native resolution and lowered the whole-frame scores slightly, more so at the larger of the two weights tried.

  • Why the hand is a blob. The record of the finger work (docs/hifi_hand.md) lists the causes on the real model: shells thicker than the gap between fingers, few Gaussians per hand, skinning that reaches across fingers, and training that poses a flat template hand while the real hand is relaxed. The last cause is the mitten experiment above.

  • Most of the close-up gain is the data. The README results compare the hi-fi model with a control that never trained on close-up rows, and say so: the large face and hand gains are mostly “the model can train on close-ups at all”. The body take loses a little in the same table.

  • Limits. The README limitations are blunt: fingers are not individually resolved in the published model (they render as blobs, sometimes in the wrong configuration), the face is soft at native 4K, and the hi-fi recipe costs a little on the body take. The toy rig works cleanly because its finger poses are exact and its fingers are three well-separated capsules. Neither is true of a real hand.

Summary#

  • A part’s detail is limited by the pixels it covers, \(n = f s / z\). At body framing a head is a few percent of the frame, and the loss cannot see its features.

  • A crop camera is the frame’s own camera with a shifted principal point and a scaled focal length. It renders one window at any resolution with exact geometry.

  • A crop loss adds full-resolution windows to the whole-frame loss. It sharpens the part a lot and costs the body score a little; measure both.

  • A close-up frame is a normal training row once a camera and a body pose are placed behind it.

  • A part with no joints is fitted to the average of its motion. Grafting joints with their own skinning weights lets it articulate, and leaves the rest of the skeleton untouched.

  • A rig is only as good as the pose that drives it.

  1. Refit the crop-loss avatar with W_CROP = 1.0 and with W_CROP = 0.05. How do the two held-out columns move?

  2. The crop loss used the true poses. Fit it again with noisy poses, cu.perturb_poses(train.poses, 3.0), for both the body frames and the crops. What happens to the head crop score, and why does a real pipeline gate its crops?

  3. Fit the rigged hand model with a wrong finger pose at training time: add 0.3 rad to every curl. Then score it with the true curls. Is it better or worse than the mitten?

Solution 1

for w in (0.05, 1.0):
    av = make_avatar()
    cu.fit(av, train, iters=ITERS, extra_loss=hifi.CropLoss(av, *crops_train, train.poses, train.trans, weight=w))
    print(w, score(av))

In our runs even the small weight gets most of the head gain, at almost no cost to the body score. A larger weight buys little more head quality and costs more body score. The crop term only has to be strong enough to shape the head texels; beyond that it competes with the body frames. The real recipe uses a small weight for the same reason.

Solution 2

noisy = cu.perturb_poses(train.poses, 3.0)
for use_crops in (False, True):
    av = make_avatar()
    loss = hifi.CropLoss(av, *crops_train, noisy, train.trans, weight=W_CROP) if use_crops else None
    cu.fit(av, train, iters=ITERS, poses=noisy, extra_loss=loss)
    print("with crops" if use_crops else "body frames only", score(av))

With poses that are wrong by a few degrees, both scores collapse, and the crop loss adds almost nothing to the head crop. A pose error of three degrees moves the head by a centimetre or more, which is a fraction of a pixel in the body frame and several pixels in the crop. The crop then teaches the texels a shifted face, each frame shifts it differently, and the average is a blur (Lesson 5b). More pixels cannot help a part that is not registered. This is why the real crop loss measures each crop’s silhouette overlap and lets badly registered crops sit out, and why the pose corrections come before the hi-fi parts.

Solution 3

wrong = hifi.HandModel(hand, rig=True)
hifi.fit_hand(wrong, hand, curls_train, hand_cams, iters=200, believed=curls_train + 0.3)
print(hifi.hand_psnr(wrong, hand, curls_test, hand_cams))

In our run its held-out PSNR is close to the mitten’s, far below the correctly driven rig. The pictures differ, though: the mitten shows a smear, and this model shows separate fingers that bend, in slightly wrong places. PSNR punishes a sharp finger in the wrong place about as hard as a blur. A rig driven by a biased tracker is a different failure from no rig, and a pixel metric alone does not tell them apart.
