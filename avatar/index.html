A 3-D avatar of me, from two phone videos

I filmed myself turning in place, then filmed a few seconds of waving. From those two clips, a model learned what I look like as about 100,000 tiny coloured blobs (3-D Gaussians) wrapped around a digital skeleton, and that skeleton can be posed any way you like. Try it below.

What you are seeing

Moves I never filmed

I never waved both hands at once, squatted or did jumping jacks on camera. Each move is written as joint rotations for the skeleton, with smooth timing and follow-through. Then the legs are solved frame by frame so that a planted foot stays flat and fixed on the floor while the body shifts its weight over it, and in jumping jacks the body dips, leaves the ground on a ballistic arc and lands to absorb it. The learned appearance follows. That is the point of an avatar: it can do things the person was never recorded doing.

Every frame is a real render

Each frame here was rendered by the model on my GPU, at nine turn angles and sixteen moments per move. The page just plays them back, so it is fast on any phone. The colours and shading are the model's own; nothing is drawn by hand.

How it works

1 · Two short videos

A slow turn in place (about 30 seconds) and a few seconds of free movement, on a phone, against a plain wall, with no markers or special suit. Plus four numbers: my height and my chest, waist and hip measurements.

2 · Tracking the body

Every frame is cut out automatically, and a standard body model (SMPL) is fitted to it using the silhouette and detected joints, nose, eyes and ears. The body's shape is fitted to views from all sides and to my measurements, and kept inside the outline of my clothes rather than inflated to fill it. Only frames used for training inform the shape, so the held-out half of the motion clip stays a fair test.

3 · Learning the appearance

Following Gaussian Shell Maps (Abdal et al., CVPR 2024), five thin shells around the body each carry a texture of 3-D Gaussians. Their colour, opacity, size and orientation are adjusted until renders match the turn and the first half of the motion clip, which takes about two minutes on one consumer GPU.

4 · Posing it

Because the Gaussians ride on shells bound to the skeleton, any new pose moves them along. Turning, waving and squatting are all just new skeleton poses.

Honest limits

Sharpest from the front

The first version tracked me well only while I faced the camera. Holding my position during the turn, fitting my shape from every side, and adding my real measurements cut the tracking error from about 40 pixels side-on to about 10 across the whole turn, so the sides and back are now learned too. Comparing my outline in a close crop around me, instead of across the whole frame, tightened the fit again, and the avatar now matches the held-out half of the motion clip noticeably better. A last per-frame touch-up of every pose, matched to my outline as the avatar itself draws it with the clothes included, made the outline fit tighter, though it did not measurably sharpen the detail. The sides and back are still softer than the front.

Poses far from anything filmed

Training on part of the waving clip fixed most of the streaks on raised arms. Surfaces the camera never saw, like the undersides of my arms and my armpits, are now coloured from their well-seen neighbours instead of being left grey, but that colour is an educated guess, and deep poses like a squat are still blurrier.

Next: a proper capture session

What is planned

Most of what still limits this avatar is the recording, not the model: one 720p phone with a guessed lens, loose clothes, and arms that were never raised during the turn. The next session uses two cameras calibrated with a checkerboard and synced with a clap, and a helper who films me holding still from every side at three heights, with my arms down and then raised. I will wear fitted clothes, record a library of the playground moves for real, and take a full set of body measurements.

A fair test

One take from that session will be set aside unopened and scored only once, after every choice about the model is final. Today's held-out score comes from later in the same clip the model learned from, and I used it to choose between versions, so it is a development number, not a sealed test.

Built for the next recording

The software for that session is already written. The core pieces were tested on synthetic data where the right answer is known exactly, against pass marks set before the code existed. Several failed at first; each failure and what changed because of it is written down. None of it has met a real recording yet.

Measuring each lens

A short video of a printed checkerboard gives each camera's focal length, centre and lens distortion, replacing today's guessed field of view. The tests taught me a practical rule: the board has to visit all four corners of the frame, or the lens model there is a guess, so the tool now warns when a corner was missed.

Lining up two cameras with a clap

A sharp clap at the start and end of every take is found in both soundtracks, which aligns the two cameras to within a fraction of a millisecond in tests, corrects for one clock running slightly fast, and pairs frames by their own timestamps (phones do not record at a perfectly steady rate).

Where the second camera stands

Holding the board where both cameras see it gives the second camera's position and angle relative to the first; laying it on the floor gives the floor. On a simulated version of the planned setup, both come out within a twentieth of a degree and a few millimetres.

Tracking from two views at once

The body is fitted to both cameras' outlines and joints together. On a simulated turn in place, the average joint error fell from 29 to 66 millimetres with one camera to 5 to 7 millimetres with two, with front, side and back views all improving by a similar amount.

Thirty-six tape measurements

Lengths such as shoulder to elbow and girths such as calf and wrist are defined on the body model the way a tape sees them, so a measured body can be fitted to them directly. On simulated bodies they pin the shape to about 5 millimetres, against 11 with the four numbers I gave last time.

A fix that helped the avatar today

Building the two-camera tracker exposed a flaw in the one I already had: it compared my outline at a scale where its own rendering of the body was blurry and full of gaps. Comparing it in a close crop around me fixed that, and re-tracking the existing videos with the fix is what made the avatar above better on the held-out clip.

How the session will run

Two cameras on tripods, about three metres away and seventy-five degrees apart, with focus, exposure and white balance locked and stabilisation off. First the checkerboard for each lens, then for both cameras together, then flat on the floor. Then a helper walks a slow circle around me at chest height, overhead and knee height while I hold still, arms out and then raised; I turn in place for both cameras; I perform each playground move for real; the helper films my face and hands up close; and one final take of mixed movement is set aside unopened as the sealed test. Every take starts and ends with a clap, and I take about thirty-six body measurements, arms and legs on both sides, twice each.

Credits

Built on Gaussian Shell Maps (Abdal et al., CVPR 2024) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., SIGGRAPH 2023), with the SMPL body model (Loper et al., 2015), torchvision's Mask R-CNN and Keypoint R-CNN (He et al., 2017) and PyTorch3D. The calibration, synchronisation and measurement tools use OpenCV, SciPy and FFmpeg. The fitting, tracking and evaluation layer that turns phone videos into this avatar is ours, written with help from Claude Code and OpenAI Codex; the code is kept in a private repository.
