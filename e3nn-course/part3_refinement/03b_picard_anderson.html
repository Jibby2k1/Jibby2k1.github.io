Picard, damping and Anderson acceleration#

What you will learn#

  • run plain Picard and watch it diverge when the map is not a contraction

  • explain damping alpha through the eigenvalues of the iteration matrix and choose alpha = 1/8

  • implement Anderson acceleration and compare evaluations to convergence

Outline#

Failure first: undamped substitution diverges on the toy. Damping is analysed through the spectrum of the linear map, then Anderson acceleration is derived as a small least-squares problem over recent residuals and compared with Picard on residual curves.

1. Why damping works: one factor per mode#

Refinement solves \(\mathbf q = T(\mathbf q)\) with \(T(\mathbf q) = \mathbf q^{\text{base}} - W(\mathbf q)\). Damped Picard moves the state only a fraction \(\alpha\) of the way to its target:

\[ \mathbf q_{k+1} = (1-\alpha)\,\mathbf q_k + \alpha\,T(\mathbf q_k). \]

For the toy’s exact map \(W(\mathbf q) = A\mathbf q\), the error can be followed exactly. Let \(\mathbf q^\star\) be the fixed point and \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k = \mathbf q_k - \mathbf q^\star\) the error. (Lesson 03a wrote \(\mathbf e\) for it; from here on the course uses \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), because \(\mathbf e_j\) is the state direction.) Since \(T(\mathbf q_k) - T(\mathbf q^\star) = -A\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k\) and \(T(\mathbf q^\star) = \mathbf q^\star\),

\[ \boldsymbol\varepsilon_{k+1} = (1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k - \alpha A\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k = \big(I - \alpha\,(I + A)\big)\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k . \]

Expand the error in eigenvectors of \(I + A\), with eigenvalues \(\mu = 1 + \lambda\) where \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\). Each mode evolves on its own, and one update multiplies it by one number:

\[ \text{factor}(\mu) = 1 - \alpha\,\mu . \]

Plotted against \(\mu\), the factor is a straight line through \((0, 1)\) with slope \(-\alpha\). A mode shrinks when its factor lies between \(-1\) and \(1\). On the default toy the eigenvalues \(\mu\) are real and positive (the script that prepares the animation’s data asserts both), so the condition for every mode at once is

\[ -1 < 1 - \alpha\,\mu < 1 \ \ \text{for all } \mu \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad 0 < \alpha < \frac{2}{\mu_{\max}} , \]

and the error shrinks per update, asymptotically, by the worst factor \(\rho(\alpha) = \max_\mu\lvert 1 - \alpha\mu\rvert\). (Lesson 03a, Section 4.1 calls this number the contraction factor \(c(\alpha)\) and gives the condition for complex \(\mu\). The clip below writes \(\rho(\alpha)\), the usual symbol for a spectral radius; it is not the density ratio \(\rho\) of the model inputs.) Two things follow. With \(\alpha = 1\) the factor is \(-\lambda\): every mode with \(\lvert\lambda\rvert > 1\) flips sign and grows, which is the divergence of plain substitution. With a very small \(\alpha\) every factor is inside the band, but the slowest mode has factor \(1 - \alpha\mu_{\min}\), close to one, so convergence is safe and slow.

Animation R2, damping and eigenvalues. A recap of the damped update for one particle, then one error mode by hand: \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon \leftarrow (1 - \alpha\mu)\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) with \(\mu = \mu_{\max}\) of the 96-sphere toy and \(\alpha = 1\), \(1/2\) and \(1/8\). It flips and grows, shrinks fast, shrinks slowly. The main plot shows the factor \(1 - \alpha\mu\) against \(\mu\), with one tick for every eigenvalue of \(I + A\) and the band \(\lvert\text{factor}\rvert < 1\) shaded. At \(\alpha = 1\) seven modes lie outside the band. As \(\alpha\) decreases the line pivots about \((0, 1)\) toward flat, and the last mode enters the band at \(\alpha = 2/\mu_{\max}\). The final panel runs the real iteration: \(\lVert\boldsymbol\varepsilon_k\rVert/\lVert\mathbf q^\star\rVert\) diverges for \(\alpha = 1\), falls fast for \(1/2\) and crawls for \(1/8\), and the measured late rates sit close to the predicted \(\rho(\alpha)\). Notice the trade: a flatter line is safer for the fast modes and slower for the slow ones. For a learned map the eigenvalues are not known in advance, which is why the course keeps the cautious \(\alpha = 1/8\). The clip is drawn on a dark stage in both page themes and has a voice-over with captions.

Takeaway. Damping does not move the fixed point; it rescales every error mode by \(1 - \alpha\mu\). The iteration converges exactly when all of those factors lie between \(-1\) and \(1\), and its speed is set by the worst one. Choosing \(\alpha\) is a trade between the largest \(\mu\), which limits how big \(\alpha\) may be, and the smallest, which wants it big.

2. Anderson acceleration: use the last defects#

Picard looks only at the current iterate. Its step follows the defect \(\mathbf f_k = T(\mathbf q_k) - \mathbf q_k\), which is zero exactly at the fixed point: \(\mathbf q_{k+1} = \mathbf q_k + \beta\,\mathbf f_k\), with the damping written \(\beta\) here. When successive defects point in nearly opposite directions, the iterates zigzag across the answer.

Anderson acceleration [Walker and Ni, 2011] uses the previous iterate too. With memory \(m = 1\), look along the line through the last two iterates,

\[ \tilde{\mathbf q}(\gamma) = \mathbf q_k - \gamma\,(\mathbf q_k - \mathbf q_{k-1}) . \]

For a linear map the defect is an affine function of the state, so the defect at that point is the same combination of the two known defects (for a nonlinear map this is an approximation):

\[ \tilde{\mathbf f}(\gamma) = \mathbf f_k - \gamma\,\Delta\mathbf f, \qquad \Delta\mathbf f = \mathbf f_k - \mathbf f_{k-1} . \]

Choose the \(\gamma\) that makes this defect as short as possible. Setting the derivative of \(\lvert\mathbf f_k - \gamma\,\Delta\mathbf f\rvert^2\) to zero gives one dot product over one squared length,

\[ \gamma = \frac{\mathbf f_k\cdot\Delta\mathbf f}{\lvert\Delta\mathbf f\rvert^2}, \]

and the update is a damped step from the mixed point along the mixed defect, \(\mathbf q_{k+1} = \tilde{\mathbf q}(\gamma) + \beta\,\tilde{\mathbf f}(\gamma)\). With memory \(m\), keep the last \(m\) differences of iterates and of defects as the columns of \(\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta F\) and fit one coefficient for each:

\[ \boldsymbol\gamma = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\gamma}\big\lVert\mathbf f_k - \Delta F\,\boldsymbol\gamma\big\rVert, \qquad \mathbf q_{k+1} = \mathbf q_k + \beta\,\mathbf f_k - (\Delta Q + \beta\,\Delta F)\,\boldsymbol\gamma . \]

This is the update of Lesson 03a, Section 5, and with \(m = 0\) it is damped Picard with \(\alpha = \beta\). The least-squares problem has only \(m\) unknowns, so its cost is negligible next to one evaluation of \(T\).

Animation R3, Anderson acceleration. The first part uses a constructed linear map with a two-component state, so that states and defects can be drawn in one plane; it runs with \(\beta = 1\), so each defect arrow (orange) reaches from an iterate to the next one. Picard follows the defect and zigzags toward the fixed point \(\mathbf q^\star\) (dashed green ring): the first two defects point nearly opposite ways. A point then slides along the line through the last two iterates while a small plot shows the length of the interpolated defect; \(\gamma\) marks its minimum, the least-squares solution above. Anderson moves to that point \(\tilde{\mathbf q}\) and steps along its defect, and after four updates its error is far smaller than Picard’s. The last part is the 96-sphere course toy with the exact map, the same damping \(\alpha = \beta = 1/8\) for every solver and a tolerance of \(10^{-10}\) on the residual \(R\): Picard needs 268 updates, Anderson 155 with \(m = 1\) and 111 with \(m = 3\). Notice that the comparison is at equal damping. Anderson is not a bigger step; it is a better direction, built from defects that were already computed. The clip is drawn on a dark stage in both page themes and has a voice-over with captions.

Takeaway. \(\boldsymbol\gamma\) is a least-squares fit: remember the last \(m\) defects and extrapolate to where they cancel. The fit is exact for a linear map and an approximation for a learned one, so the damping \(\beta\) stays. Each update still costs one evaluation of \(T\), one pass of the surrogate over every particle surface, and that count is what Anderson reduces.
